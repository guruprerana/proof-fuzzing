You are converting a natural-language olympiad proof into a compact implication skeleton.

Input:
  problem_statement:
  {{PROBLEM_STATEMENT}}

  proof_text:
  {{PROOF_TEXT}}

Goal:
  Produce a semi-formal proof skeleton in terms of implications, equivalences, conjunctions, disjunctions, atomic English math statements, and references to other implications.

Core requirements:
  1. Preserve the proof strategy and source order unless a small reorder makes dependencies clearer.
  2. Use numbered labels impl1, impl2, impl3, ...
  3. Each impl block has premises, optional references to earlier implications, an arrow => or <=>, and a conclusion.
  4. Keep each conclusion local. Do not conclude the final theorem in the middle of the proof unless the block is explicitly a final aggregator.
  5. Prefer many small implications over one large implication.
  6. If the proof uses a named theorem, standard fact, or hidden bridge, make it its own implication when possible.
  7. Aggregator implications are allowed, but they should only combine already stated local claims.
  8. Do not invent a new proof. Formalize the proof actually given.
  9. Do not hide proof steps in global prose. Use implications.
  10. Use atomic English math statements rather than polished exposition.
  11. If the proof states and proves a reusable lemma, represent it as a named lemma block with internal implication labels, then use only its exported statement in the main proof.
  12. Ignore dataset metadata lines and final-answer wrappers unless they contain mathematical proof content.

Output format:
  impl1:
  atomic premise,
  atomic premise,
  implX,
  implY
  =>
  atomic conclusion.

Optional lemma-module format:
  lemma L1_name:
  statement:
    atomic lemma statement.
  proof:
    L1.impl1:
    atomic premise
    =>
    atomic conclusion.

    L1.impl2:
    L1.impl1
    =>
    atomic conclusion.
  exports:
    L1.statement.

  main proof implications may reference:
    L1.statement instantiated with the relevant variables.


Guidance for choosing implication size:
  Good:
    one implication for a definition;
    one implication for a modular equivalence;
    one implication for applying a counting bound;
    one implication for passing from infinitely many objects of one kind to infinitely many objects of another kind.

  Bad:
    one implication for an entire paragraph with several mathematical moves;
    one implication that concludes the theorem before the final assembly;
    one implication whose premises are only "impl3, impl4, impl5" but the necessary variables are not visible nearby.

  WLOG and case-reuse guidance:
    If a proof assumes without loss of generality that x < y for a construction, and later applies that construction to a transformed pair, include a separate implication that relabels the transformed pair by smaller/larger value.
    If a case splits into an immediate branch and a branch that reuses an earlier construction, represent the branches separately before aggregating.


Few-shot example 1: modular reduction

Source proof fragment:
  Let k be a positive integer. We need n such that A_n = k and k divides n+2024.
  The divisibility condition is equivalent to n congruent to -2024 modulo k.

Skeleton:
  impl1:
  k is a positive integer,
  n is an integer
  =>
  (k divides n+2024 <=> n is congruent to -2024 modulo k).

  impl2:
  k is a positive integer,
  A_n = k,
  k divides n+2024
  =>
  A_n divides n+2024.


Few-shot example 2: interval contains a residue

Source proof fragment:
  The interval I_k = [s_k, s_{k+1}-1] has length L_k = s_{k+1}-s_k.
  If L_k >= k, then I_k contains a complete set of residues modulo k.

Skeleton:
  impl1:
  k is a positive integer,
  I_k = [s_k, s_{k+1}-1],
  L_k = s_{k+1}-s_k,
  L_k >= k
  =>
  I_k contains at least one integer in every residue class modulo k.


Few-shot example 3: final theorem should be delayed

  impl8:

Better skeleton:

  there are infinitely many positive integers k such that for each such k there exists a positive integer n with A_n = k and A_n divides n+2024

Few-shot example 5: proved lemma as a module

Source proof fragment:
  Lemma: Let H be a tree with M vertices and maximum degree at most 3. If M >= 3K-1, then there is an edge whose removal leaves both components with at least K vertices.
  Proof: Assume not. Orient each edge toward the large component. A finite tree orientation has a sink. The three branches at the sink each have at most K-1 vertices, giving M <= 3K-2, contradiction.
  Later, apply the lemma to G with K=6n.

Skeleton:
  lemma L1_tree_partition:
  statement:
  H is a tree with M vertices and maximum degree at most 3,
  K is a positive integer,
  M >= 3K-1
  =>
  there exists an edge e of H such that removing e splits H into two components each having at least K vertices.

  proof:
  L1.impl1:
  no edge e of H splits H into two components each having at least K vertices
  =>
  every edge e of H has at least one side with at most K-1 vertices.

  L1.impl2:
  finite tree H has an acyclic orientation
  =>
  H has a sink vertex S.

  L1.impl3:
  S is a sink vertex,
  H has maximum degree at most 3,
  every incident branch at S has at most K-1 vertices
  =>
  M <= 3K-2.

  L1.impl4:
  M >= 3K-1,
  M <= 3K-2
  =>
  contradiction.

  exports:
  L1.statement.

  impl_main:
  G is a tree with 18n vertices and maximum degree at most 3,
  K_1 = 6n,
  18n >= 3K_1-1,
  L1.statement instantiated with H=G, M=18n, K=K_1
  =>
  there exists an edge e_1 of G such that removing e_1 splits G into two components each having at least 6n vertices.


Final instruction:
  Output only the implication skeleton. Do not add explanatory paragraphs.
